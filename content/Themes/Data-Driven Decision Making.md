## Definition
The organisational practice of grounding business decisions in verified evidence and quantifiable insight rather than intuition, seniority, or assumption — positioned in the series as a survival competency for SMBs competing against better-resourced rivals.

## Why it matters
The series cites Harvard Business Review research that data-driven organisations are 5% more productive and 6% more profitable than competitors relying on gut instinct. For resource-constrained SMBs, this productivity gap is framed as a matter of competitive survival, not merely operational optimisation.

## How it works
- **DYOR as entry mindset** — "Do Your Own Research" before making decisions with significant consequence, especially in the absence of trusted third-party validation
- **Moving from anecdote to evidence** — replacing assumption-based product or marketing decisions with tested, measured alternatives
- **Democratising data access** — ensuring employees at all levels, not just leadership, have access to relevant data to inform daily decisions
- **Balancing data with intuition** — the series explicitly notes that data-driven culture does not eliminate the value of human creativity and judgement, only supplements it with evidence

## Examples from the books
- From [[Data Insights and Analytics - A Guidebook for SMB Leaders]] (Ch. 1): DYOR's origin in cryptocurrency investment culture is traced and generalised to business decision-making broadly, warning against taking information at face value from untrusted sources.
- From [[Data Insights and Analytics - A Guidebook for SMB Leaders]] (Ch. 4): a fitness studio example shows a data-driven organisation analysing customer feedback and usage patterns to prioritise product features, rather than a product manager assuming what customers want.
