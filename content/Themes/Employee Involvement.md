## Definition
The practice of actively involving employees — particularly those closest to the affected processes — in the planning, design, and decision-making of a change initiative, rather than presenting change as a top-down directive.

## Why it matters
Across all three volumes, the single most consistent driver of both successful adoption and failed transformation is whether frontline employees felt genuinely heard. Involvement converts passive recipients of change into active co-owners, which measurably reduces resistance and surfaces practical insights leadership alone would miss.

## How it works
- **Early involvement** — bringing employees into planning before decisions are finalised, not after
- **Structured feedback mechanisms** — workshops, interviews, surveys, and anonymous suggestion channels
- **Cross-functional teams** — assembling representatives from different departments (and even seemingly unrelated ones, like HR or accounting) to widen the pool of insight
- **Visible response to input** — closing the loop by acting on feedback, which builds trust for future involvement efforts
- **Ownership through co-design** — employees who help design a solution are measurably more likely to champion its adoption

## Examples from the books
- From [[AI and Digital Transformation - A Guidebook for SMB Leaders]] (Ch. 8): Solpaque's operations manager runs stakeholder workshops before automating inventory management, surfacing packaging and wiring issues that would have derailed a top-down rollout.
