## Definition
A deliberate plan for crafting and delivering change-related messages that is aligned with organisational values, tailored to audience diversity, and delivered through an appropriate mix of channels.

## Why it matters
The content of a message and the style and channel of delivery are equally important. Poorly communicated change, even when well-planned, generates resistance, confusion, and distrust. Effective communication transforms employees from passive recipients into active participants.

## How it works
**Crafting the narrative:**
1. Start with a thorough analysis of why the change is needed
2. Clearly articulate the current state, using data and real examples
3. Paint a compelling vision of the desired future state
4. Address risks honestly — both the risk of changing AND of not changing
5. Align the narrative with the organisation's values and culture

**Key risks to communicate proactively:**
- Job insecurity — acknowledge concerns; outline reskilling plans
- Workflow disruption — set realistic timelines; use From→To diagrams
- Skill gaps — explain training programs available
- Resistance and morale — invite open dialogue
- Uncertainty — provide roadmaps and milestones
- Cognitive biases (loss aversion, status quo preference) — name them openly
- Lack of inclusivity — ensure all levels receive relevant information
- Overconfidence bias in leadership — encourage realistic, balanced narratives

**Channel selection:**
- Mix digital and face-to-face channels to reach diverse audiences
- Use two-way channels (forums, feedback platforms) not just broadcast
- Adjust communication style for cultural context (see [[Cultural Intelligence]])

## Examples
- From [[Change Management - A Guidebook for SMB Leaders]]: CEO Sarah Punkett at PunkVision used a town hall storytelling approach, weaving data with narrative, then followed up through multiple digital and face-to-face channels with two-way feedback — resulting in employees becoming champions of the change.
